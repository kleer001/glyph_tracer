Is the landing flash doing anything?

The same recording twice, one knob apart. A piece lands on its own colour, shoves the line in front of it, and its cell goes. On the right, the cell it landed on floods with more of its own colour for a fifth of a second.

Everything else on this page has landed. This one is built and drawn here, and is not in the game: it is one line in the compositor away, or one deletion.

What the flash is

A radial bloom in the colour of the cell that fired, composited with lighter, loudest at the landing and spent 180 ms later. It draws over the ground and under everything else, so a beam and a piece both pass in front of it.

A bloom rather than a filled cell, because the tiles have rounded corners and sit in gutters: a square of added light would put four bright corners in the gaps between cells, which is precisely where nothing happened. It reaches __SPREAD__ of a cell width, which keeps it off the neighbours — a wider one lights up cells that did not do anything.

The case against: the palette does not take it evenly

The cell above is green, and green is the best case. Adding a colour to itself only brightens it where the colour has headroom left, and this palette leaves wildly different amounts. Every live colour at the flash's loudest instant:

Lift is in relative luminance, and the spread is sevenfold. A green cell floods; a red one barely moves, because red's own channel is already at 243 and adding more of it clips on the first frame, leaving only the two channels that are not the colour to do anything. The recording above is showing the flash at close to its strongest.

That is worse than being weak. A feedback signal is supposed to report the event, and this one reports the hue of the tile the event happened on: the same activation is a flood on cyan and a shrug on red. If it stays, the fix is to flash a fixed colour rather than the cell's own — even across the palette, at the cost of the thing that made it worth trying, which was the cell filling up with more of itself.

It also arrives late to a job already done. The landing is now marked by a freeze, the collapse by a turn that quickens down a chain, and the ability itself by a beam. A fourth voice saying here, this cell, now is the one that has to justify itself.

The case for it

Those three all mark the consequence. The freeze happens on the beat before the fire, the beam leaves after it and the collapse comes later still; none of them brightens the cell at the moment of arrival. The flash is the only one that lands on the instant itself, which is also the only moment a still frame of this game would show.

And the board sat down into a tray

The one that is about the mechanic rather than the feel.

Tiles used to float on the paper, which made a cleared cell a hole in the page. The grid now sits in a recessed tray, and the same hole is a hole in the board. For a game whose whole premise is that the board drains, that is the difference between emptiness reading as progress and emptiness reading as nothing rendered.

The tray is six numbers under well in data/gloss.json: how far it reaches past the outermost cells, how much darker than the paper it is, and the shadow cast inward from its rim.

It matters more here than it would elsewhere, and the palette is why: a yellow tile against the bare paper measures 1.02:1. They are the same luminance. Before the tray, the gutter and the tile's own shadow were the only things separating a pale cell from a gap where a cell used to be.

What is still on the shelf

  • Move preview — show what a swap would do before committing to it. Normally expensive; here the engine already resolves a swap on a copy without committing, and a deterministic cascade is the ideal case for it.
  • A particle burst on a clear — needs a layer and a clock, and the clock is now on the frame.
  • Feedback for a dud swap — a swap that achieves nothing currently produces nothing.
  • Segment welding — priced cheap on the shelf, and it is not. Welding consecutive moves into one envelope means rebuilding the timeline as per-piece envelopes instead of phases, and it pulls against the two changes above, which exist to make each link a distinct beat.

How these were made

Both recordings are the real engine resolving the real rules. The frame harness draws one named effect at an elapsed time it is handed and runs no clock of its own, so a capture is the same frame every run — which is what makes one recording worth comparing against the next. The two above differ by a single number, substituted at the network layer so nothing in the game moved between the passes.

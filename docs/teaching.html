Glyph Tracer · Level structure

Teaching a game with no tutorial text

There are sixteen glyphs but only four primitives. Side count is the verb, fill is the magnitude, the inner mark is the targeting, nesting is the range — so the curriculum is not sixteen lessons, it is four, and the shapes carry the rest. What the levels have to teach is the primitives, one at a time, in the order the rules depend on them — plus the two things total reach adds: that the whole board is always in play, and that a shove leaves holes worth moving into.

What the levels teach, in dependency order

Each rung leans on the one above it, so this is the order they were laid out in.
01A glyph on its own color activatesThe single rule everything else modifies. Nothing can be shown before this.
02Swapping moves glyphs, not cellsBackgrounds are terrain. Until this lands, players read the board as movable tiles.
03Any two cells swapReach is total. The board is not a set of local choices, it is one field — which is what makes the later acts about picking rather than finding.
04The cell is gone afterwardNo refill. Sets up the whole economy — material is finite and spending it is the game.
05A budget, and a targetTurns the drain into a level. Meaningless before the player knows what a swap yields.
06Activating shoves neighborsThe first ability. Needs the rules above, because a shove is only legible once you know why a cell vanished.
07A shoved glyph can activateThe combo. This is the moment the game stops being one move at a time.
08A shove leaves an empty cell behindOnly shoving makes them — an activated cell dies, it does not empty. So this rung is unreachable until the one above it lands, and moving a piece onto one is a real move the board did not have before.
09Shape tells you the abilityOnly worth teaching once shoves exist — before that there is nothing for a verb to differ about.
10Sinks: edge, hole, eaterOne rule, three faces. Introduce the edge first — it is always visible and always present.
11The visible payoff sorts almost nothingLast, and only last. Thirty swaps show two matches and one of them pays; that reads as a betrayal rather than a puzzle until the habit is twenty levels old.

What total reach changed

Measured, not assumed — the run above is built on these.

Letting any two cells swap does not simply make the game easier. It moves which dial the difficulty is on, and it changes what a trap is.

Fresh board, 6 colorsNeighbors onlyAny two cells
Productive openings19.4234.2
Swaps tied for the best visible payoff4.526.4
Best visible payoff available1.872.00

Palette size no longer decides whether you can match. Under the old rule the best payoff on a fresh six-color board averaged 1.87, meaning a double hit was sometimes not on offer at all, and at eight colors it fell to 1.70. Under total reach it is exactly 2.00 at every palette size from two colors to eight — a double is always somewhere. That is why Act I is flat at 12.0 whatever palette it uses, and why palette only starts costing anything in Act II: it stopped gating whether you find a match and now only gates how far the chain runs after you do.

The dials, ranked by what they are worth over a six-swap level. Palette across 2–8 colors moves the yield by 8.5 cells (22.1 down to 13.6); eater density across 0–50% moves it by 2.6; pusher density across 0–100% moves it by 3.7 and saturates early — going from half the board pushing to all of it buys 0.7. So palette remains the difficulty curve, eaters are a usable second dial, and pusher density is close to a switch rather than a slider.

A trap is now a haystack rather than a decoy. On an annealed board about thirty swaps tie for the best visible payoff instead of three or four. The generator still lands a single solver every time and the best line is still worth ten to fifteen times the tempting move, but the mechanism has changed: the player is no longer choosing between a lure and a line, they are choosing one of thirty moves that look identical, twenty-nine of which are duds. Picking among them averages 5–6 cells against a best of 22–29.

That is why tools/trapBoards.js now reports how many swaps tie and what picking among them is worth. deception is measured against the worst swap in that tied pool, which was a fair summary when the pool held three moves and flatters the generator when it holds thirty.

Setting the target

One policy, applied to the measured mean for each configuration.

Every target above is the measured six-swap mean for that configuration multiplied by the level's factor, rounded. The factor carries the difficulty curve here, and the swap budget is held at six across the run so that it does. The factor is the design and the mean is a measurement, so when a rule changes and the means move, the factors stay and the targets follow them.

StageFactorEffect
Teaching a rule0.33 – 0.55Effectively unlosable. The player is watching a mechanic, not being tested on it.
Practising it0.60 – 0.96Clean play passes; wasted swaps do not.
Testing it1.00 – 1.30Above the mean, so the level cannot be passed with six unrelated singles. Requires a chain.

No dead boards to design around. The sweeps found zero boards with no legal swap at any configuration in this document, so no level in the run needs a reshuffle rule or a soft-lock escape.

When to spring the trap

Act IV is last for a reason that is not difficulty pacing. A trap works by punishing a habit, and the habit it punishes — take the swap that shows the most immediate matches — is one the first three acts spend twenty levels installing. Every Act I level rewards it, because with no pushers on the board the visible payoff is the real payoff — and under total reach a maximal-looking swap is always on offer, so the habit never once fails the player before Act IV. Acts II and III keep rewarding it while quietly making it an approximation.

Sprung in Act I it would likely read as an unfair board; sprung in Act IV the guess is that it reads as the game revealing what it has been about. Which of those a player feels is a playtest question, not something the sweeps can answer. Same board, different meaning, and the only difference is how much practice the player has at being right.

Authoring the levels

Act I and II boards can be random. Act III and IV need generating. 
# a mild trap for level 20 — a real line, but not a board wipe
node tools/trapBoards.js --colors 6 --target 12 --payoff 8 --json lvl20.json

# level 23: the inverted case, tighter palette pressure
node tools/trapBoards.js --colors 8 --target 14 --payoff 10 --iters 4000 --json lvl23.json

# confirm a hand-tuned config's yield before setting its target
node tools/swapBudget.js --colors 6 --pushers 0.5 --blockers 0.125 --trials 300
--target

Caps how much a bigger cascade keeps scoring. Low for teaching levels so the payoff stays legible; high for finales.

--payoff

Floor on the best line. Guarantees the level is winnable by the intended route.

--pushers / --blockers

Set to zero for Act I. The generator honors them, so an act's config is also its authoring config.

solvers

Reported per board. Keep it at 1 for teaching levels — a single answer is what makes the lesson unambiguous.

Left out

⊘ Void sits at levels 19 and 20 in Act III rather than in an act of its own, because it is the mirror of a push and reads against the shove rules. ⬠ Wild has no level in this run. It fires a random one of sixteen subsets, so a board carrying one has no fixed best line to author toward, and a taught level wants one unambiguous answer. Generated and endless play do not, which is where a wild would cost nothing.

The upgrade tree sits outside this run entirely. This is the curriculum for the base rules; a progression layer on top of it is a separate structure and a separate document.

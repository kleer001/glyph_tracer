Glyph Tracer · Board generation

Trapping the greedy read

A player cannot run a cascade in their head. All they can see is a swap's immediate payoff — does it land one glyph on its own color, or two? So a board becomes a puzzle at exactly the moment that visible number stops predicting the outcome.

The measurement

Every legal swap on the board, resolved to completion, then compared three ways.
Best line 31 Cells cleared by the strongest swap on the board, cascade included.
Greedy pick 2 Cleared by the swap with the highest visible payoff — what a surface read takes.
Typical swap 1 Median across all 263 productive swaps. Outside the line, you clear what you aimed at.

Out of 40 cells. The two derived figures the generator optimizes — deception (best minus greedy) and spread (best minus median) — are differences, not totals. On this board deception is 29, which is the distance between 31 and 2.

The third number, solvers, counts how many swaps reach 80% of best. Here it is 1: there is exactly one line and everything else is noise.

Nothing on the surface separates them. Both moves show two matches; one clears two cells and the other clears thirty-one. The difference lives entirely in which glyphs shove, which absorb, and what is downrange of them.

Lowercase = cell background color, UPPERCASE = glyph color, ✳ advances all four of its lines outward by one, and ▮ absorbs whatever is shoved into it and stays put. Those are the two abilities in this board's mix. Turn --rotators or --voids up and ↻ ↺ and ◌ join them. Letters stand in for colors; the palette is not decided here.

Every swap here presents the player with the same number — two immediate matches — and they clear anything from 2 to 31. Sixteen of the thirty-eight clear exactly two and stop. The visible payoff is constant across the whole group and carries no information whatsoever about which one to take: picking among them at random averages 5.6 cells against a best of 31. Why a board can be read wrong at all

One rule, three sinks.

A shove travels down a line of glyphs and ends at a sink. Every sink behaves identically: the glyph at the front of the line is eaten, everything behind it advances one. There are three — the board edge, a cell already destroyed, and a glyph that eats what is pushed into it.

Take a row reading A B C D, where A eats. D lands on its own color and activates, pushing left. What is left is A C _ ×: A stays, B is consumed, C takes B's place, and D's cell is destroyed by the activation that started the whole thing. A line only advances intact when it ends on a live empty cell.

_ is a live but empty cell — a glyph shoved there would land on it and could match. × is a cell that no longer exists.

This is what makes the board unreadable at a glance. Whether a swap cascades depends on where the sinks are relative to the pushers — information that is on the board but several inferences deep, and nothing like as available as counting immediate matches.

Method

Simulated annealing over single-cell mutations. tools/trapBoards.js
01 / Score

Resolve every swap

For each legal swap, copy the board, apply it, and settle it completely — activations, shoves, chain shoves, further activations. Record what the player would see and what actually happens.

02 / Mutate

Change one cell

Re-roll a background color, re-roll a glyph color, or change what kind of glyph it is. Backgrounds and glyphs are kept unequal so no board opens with a free match.

03 / Anneal

Cool toward the trap

Keep improvements; keep some regressions early and fewer later. Boards below the minimum payoff are penalized rather than thrown out, so the search can walk through bad ground to reach good ground.

04 / Cap

Aim at a size

Past --target, a larger cascade stops adding score. Without it the search runs away and produces board-wipes; with it you get the trap you asked for.

The objective, in full:

score = 3.0 × min(deception, target)
      + 1.0 × min(spread,    target)
      + 0.5 × min(best,      target)
      − 2.0 × max(0, solvers − 1)
      − 8.0 × max(0, payoff − best)     # gate, walkable rather than fatal

Deception is weighted triple because it is the thing that makes a board a puzzle rather than a lottery. The solver penalty is what forces a single answer: without it the search happily produces boards with six equally good lines, which is generous rather than interesting.

Why nothing here rolls a die

What this method needs from the rules.

Every score on this page is a property of the board. That only holds because every glyph resolves the same way every time: the abilities are in src/board.js's fire, and none of them consults a random number. Seeded randomness deals a board; from there the board is the whole answer.

An ability with no fixed outcome would take that away. Its board would have no fixed best line, so deception, spread and solvers would all be samples rather than measurements, and the annealer would be optimizing a number that is not a property of the thing it is mutating. There would also be nothing for an author to author toward, and nothing for a player to have been right about.

A typical swap clears exactly 1 cell on every board — the one the player aimed at, with nothing following. Against that floor the line is worth 11 to 15 times the greedy alternative, and on all four the lure and the line are visually indistinguishable.

At --target 12 the same search yields a best line of 14 against a greedy pick of 2 — a smaller trap that still resolves to one answer. The dial goes the other way too. Adding the rearrangers back in does not cost the method anything: at --rotators 0.125 --voids 0.125 the search still lands a 27-cell line against a greedy pick of 2, with one solver.

Running it

node tools/trapBoards.js                          # one 6-color board
node tools/trapBoards.js --boards 4               # four of them
node tools/trapBoards.js --target 12              # a modest trap, not a wipe
node tools/trapBoards.js --colors 4 --payoff 10   # tighter palette, higher floor
node tools/trapBoards.js --blockers 0.25          # more anchors on the board
node tools/trapBoards.js --voids 0.125            # add inward-pullers
node tools/trapBoards.js --rotators 0.125         # add glyphs that turn their neighbors
node tools/trapBoards.js --adjacent-only          # restrict to neighbor swaps
node tools/trapBoards.js --json boards.json       # dump for a real prototype

src/board.js holds the board, the shove and the settle loop, shared with tools/swapBudget.js. Board JSON carries backgrounds, glyph colors, the kind in each cell and both marked swaps, so a generated level can be loaded straight into a build and checked against its own solution.

Board shown is data/example_board.json, seed 20260825, six colors, 50% pulse, 12.5% anchor, 3000 annealing steps, any two cells — regenerate it with node tools/trapBoards.js --iters 3000 --json boards.json. Rules and measurements in SPEC.md.

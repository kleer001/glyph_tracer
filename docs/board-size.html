How big should the board be?

Any two cells may be swapped, which puts 780 pairs in front of a player on a 5×8 board. Around thirty of them tie for the best payoff anyone can see, so the move a player picks out of that tie is close to a guess. Shrinking the board is the obvious fix. It works, and it is not free — the same thing that makes the choice legible takes the runway the ability layer needs. Nothing here is decided. This page is the measurement, so the decision can be made on it.

The quantity that matters

Reproduce with node tools/boardShapes.js.

A player cannot simulate a cascade. What they can see is a swap’s immediate payoff, so the question is how many swaps share the best one on offer. At three or four, picking between them is a decision. At thirty it is a lottery, and a trap built on it punishes a coin toss rather than a habit.

That count tracks pairs ÷ colors² closely enough to design against. Board size and palette size are not two dials, they are one.

The ability layer is what a small board spends. A shove on 5×8 travels 1.98 cells before it meets something; on 4×4 it travels 1.19. The deepest cascade in a level falls from 2.15 steps to 1.37 — which is barely a chain. The rules put the combo layer at the centre of the game on purpose, and 4×4 is the shape that has nowhere to put it.

Small boards run out of moves. A 4×4 level spends 0.66 of its six swaps on turns where no productive swap exists at all. That is not a soft lock — no configuration measured here produced a board with no legal swap, at any size — but it is a wasted tap the player did not cause. On 5×5 and larger it rounds to zero.

Traps get smaller with the board. The generator finds a single solver on every shape it was given. What changes is the ceiling: a best line of 7–11 cells on 4×4 against 22–29 on 5×8. The structure survives; the spectacle does not.

The budget stopped biting

Marginal cells gained by swap number.

Six swaps was chosen because that is where the yield curve flattened. It flattened there under a rule where only neighbors could swap. With total reach a useful pair is always somewhere on a large board, so the curve stopped flattening and the budget became an arbitrary cap rather than the point of exhaustion.

This is the one measurement that argues for a small board on its own terms. On 4×4 the board is genuinely spent by swap seven — the tail is 0.3 cells — so a six-swap budget describes the game rather than truncating it. On 5×8 swaps seven through twelve are still worth 7.5 cells, half a level again.

The tied count falls to 6.1 at twelve colors and then climbs again, which looks like the dial reversing and is not. Past twelve, some boards no longer offer a double hit anywhere — 3% at sixteen colors, 10% at twenty — and on those boards the best visible payoff is a single, so the tie is counted among the far more numerous single-hit swaps. The measure changes meaning rather than the board getting better. Twelve is where the palette lever runs out on this board size.

What is actually being traded

Every route to a legible choice costs something different, and none of them is free:

4×4 at six colors buys the smallest tie pool and the only honest six-swap budget, and pays with the cascade — a 1.37 deepest chain, a 1.19 shove runway, a trap ceiling of eleven cells, and a wasted turn in two levels out of three. It is also square, and a phone in portrait is not.

5×5 at eight colors gets the tie pool to 5.9 while keeping three quarters of the shove runway and losing no turns. It clears 40% of the board per level, which keeps the drain readable. The budget half-bites: a 3.7 tail.

5×8 at twelve colors keeps the full runway and the current board, and fixes only the tie pool. It does not fix the budget, and it costs the six colors the glyph grammar and the specimen plate are drawn against.

A fourth route exists and is not measured here: keep the board and the palette, and change what the player is choosing between rather than how many things they are choosing between. The tie pool is only a problem because immediate payoff is the only signal a board gives. A glyph whose ability is legible from its drawing is a second signal, and the run in teaching.html spends eight levels teaching it. How much of the tie pool that actually resolves is a question for a playtest, not a sweep.

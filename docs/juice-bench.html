Glyph Tracer · visual research

What the board could do while it drains

Eleven agents read the sister repos, the studio shelves and the field. Two questions they could only guess at were measured instead. Everything below is a price tag, not a plan.

The game as it stands

Swap two pieces. One that lands on its own colour activates, fires an ability, and its cell goes away. No gravity, no refill — the board drains, and the holes are the score. One swap can set off a chain a dozen links deep.

What plays today: linear tweens, a stagger wave across shoved pieces, move-then-clear split beats, and a shrink-with-a-turn on a destroyed cell. Everything is a candidate to sit on top of that.

Measurement 01

The keyline is the contrast mechanism

Four colours on the board, and every glyph wears one of them on a tile wearing another. That makes six pairs that all have to stay legible. Three of them don't — not by a little.

WCAG contrast · live palette W3C G207 sets 3:1 as the floor for graphics that carry meaning.

Red, green and blue are near-identical in luminance and differ only in hue. What separates a red glyph from a green tile is the black keyline, which measures 4.62:1 to 5.27:1 against every colour on the board. It is not a finishing touch — it is the whole mechanism, and thinning it for a cleaner look breaks three pairs outright.

Yellow against the paper background is 1.02:1. A yellow tile and an empty cell are the same luminance. Only the gutter and the tile's own shadow tell them apart — which is a problem for a game where noticing the holes is the point.

Measurement 02

The rotations are fine. The letters collide.

Four of the twelve glyphs are one letterform at four rotations, and the received wisdom says rotated letters confuse players. Rendering each glyph as a solid silhouette and comparing every pair by overlap says otherwise.

Silhouette overlap · intersection over union Higher means harder to tell apart at a glance.

The four rotations don't appear anywhere near the top — A is asymmetric enough that turning it makes four separate shapes. The collisions are between different letters that happen to share a build: H and R are both tall and two-stemmed, and R against its own mirror is the old b/d problem.

Ink coverage runs 5.2% of the cell for the dot up to 9.1% for H — a 1.8× spread in weight across a set meant to read as one family.

Candidate 01 · cheap

Easing

Linear motion reads as mechanical, because nothing starts and stops instantly. This is the largest change in feel per line edited, and the sister game already runs the curve.

Candidate 02 · cheap

Hit stop

Freeze everything for a beat at the instant a piece lands on its colour, then resume. The apparatus already exists — a phase can already hold still. It costs a number.

Four-link chain, with and without The lower board holds 70 ms at each landing.

Candidate 03 · cheap

Escalation across the chain

A twelve-link chain plays every link identically today. Scaling the feedback with the link index is the mechanic's own drama, and nothing else on this list produces it. The cap matters as much as the ramp: past a point, more intensity stops reading as more.

Eight links · flat vs ramped Ramp raises hold, shrink speed and turn together, then caps at link six.

Candidate 04 · cheap

Sit the board in a well

Tiles float on the page today, so a cleared cell is a hole in the page. Recess the grid and the same hole becomes a hole in the board — a state rather than an absence of rendering. For a game whose premise is that the board drains, one shadow decides whether emptiness reads as progress or as a bug.

Candidate 08 · moderate

A burst on the clear

Pooled particles as filled arcs in the colour of the piece that died. Needs a new layer and a clock on the frame, which the renderer doesn't carry yet.

Motion is polar about the cell that threw it — a fixed angle and a growing radius, so there is no second axis for a force to act on. A burst that arced downward would be the only thing on screen obeying gravity, which this board does not have. Drag pulls each grain toward a slow terminal creep rather than to a stop. Every knob is on a slider in dev/burst.html.

The whole shelf, priced

cheap is lines inside a layer that already exists. moderate is a new layer or a new field on the frame. expensive is a new render path.

  1. 01

    Easing on every tween

    cheap

    Cubic ease-out for a piece arriving, ease-in-out for a cell dying. treasure_trash already runs both, plus an accelerate-then-cruise curve for momentum pushes.

  2. 02

    Hit stop on activation

    cheap

    A hold in the phase list at each landing. Durations in circulation cluster at 50–200 ms; the frame counts quoted around fighting games are not reliably sourced, so tune it rather than trust them.

  3. 03

    Segment welding

    cheap

    Consecutive steps restart the clock every beat. Merging a run into one envelope removes the stutter — exactly what a twelve-link chain needs.

  4. 04

    Escalation across the chain

    cheap

    Available today, before any new layer exists, because it is a function of values already passed to phaseOf.

  5. 05

    Inset the board into a well

    cheap

    One inset shadow in the ground layer. The cheapest change that makes emptiness read as progress.

  6. 06

    A flash on activation

    cheap

    globalCompositeOperation = 'lighter' is a state flag, effectively free. Needs elapsed time on the frame first.

  7. 07

    Move preview

    moderate

    Normally expensive; here the engine already resolves a swap on a copy without committing it. A deterministic cascade is the ideal case. The argument is Into the Breach's — sacrifice cool ideas for clarity, every time.

  8. 08

    Particle burst on a clear

    moderate

    Cost is in globalAlpha changes and beginPath/arc pairs, not geometry. Published per-frame figures are poorly sourced — measure.

  9. 09

    Feedback for a dud swap

    moderate

    A swap that achieves nothing currently produces nothing at all. Something is owed here.

  10. 10

    Glow and bloom

    expensive

    shadowBlur is among the slowest canvas operations; layered strokes are the cheap approximation. A centred halo needs only zero offsets and an opaque shadow colour — a transparent one is what renders nothing.

Ruled out, with reasons

  • Chromatic aberration, ripple, shockwave. Canvas 2D has no displacement primitive. They need getImageData, which stalls the pipeline. WebGL or nothing.
  • Full-canvas trails. The renderer reassigns canvas.width every frame to track device pixel ratio, which wipes the buffer the trick depends on.
  • Anything built on gravity or refill. Most match-3 technique is about falling and cascading. This board does neither.
  • Ambient occlusion in the gutters. Invisible at tile size, muddy where it isn't.
  • Parallax. A turn-based board has nothing to parallax against.

One disagreement worth settling on purpose

The research split cleanly, and both halves are defensible.

  • Decorate the clear. A burst, a flash, a pop at every activation — standard across the genre, and what makes a clear feel like a reward.
  • Let the absence speak. Put nothing on an empty cell. Where emptiness is the score, silence reads louder than confetti, and effects piled on a hole compete with the thing they celebrate.

They reconcile in one reading: juice the dying piece, leave the hole alone. The piece is an event and can be loud. The hole is a state and should be quiet.

And one constraint sits over all of it: this game has no audio. Whatever the hook is, it has to read silently, at phone size, in about three seconds — which puts the weight on the two things a still frame can show. The chain building, and the board draining.
